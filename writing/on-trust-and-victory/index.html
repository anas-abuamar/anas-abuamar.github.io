On Trust and Victory: Lessons from the Vertical World

  1. The Anatomy of Trust
  2. The Climb That Changed Me
  3. What the Rock Taught Me
Written at UWC-USA in May 2024, after project week that March.

Trust is the rope we weave together, when one falls, the other holds, and in that tension, we both rise

If anyone ever asks me what experience taught me the purest form of trust, I’d answer without hesitation: rock climbing.

During last March’s project week in Socorro, NM and Southeastern Arizona; Cochise Stronghold & Santa Catalina Mountains, both Pueblo (native) land, I learned that when you’ve completed your safety checks and hear the call “Climb on,” the world shrinks to just three things: you, the rope, and the rock. Nothing compares to that moment of surrender, when your life depends on the figure eight knots you’ve tied, the belayer’s focus, and your own shaking feet and fingers.

The Anatomy of Trust

In the trip, I mastered the rituals of survival:

  • Tying the rope into my harness with a figure-eight knot,
  • Carefully, setting up the belay device,
  • The sacred call-and-response of climbing commands ( “On belay?” “Belay on!” ),
  • The PBUS technique (Pull, Brake, Under, Slide), a reminder for keeping others alive.

These weren’t just skills; they were a pledge. To clip the rope wrong was to break something someone had handed you. To lose focus was to fall.

The Climb That Changed Me

I’ll never forget the 5.10 route that broke me open. Three meters up, missing two holds, muscles screaming, I hung there like a prayer, resting between each attempt. Below me, voices wove a net of encouragement: “Yalla!” (my own ragged cry), “Take your time,” “You’re stronger than you think.”

Seven times I lunged for the next hold. Seven times I peeled away from the rock. The sun dipped low, painting the canyon in gold, as my hands grew raw with effort and excitement. On the final attempt, I looked down and saw two belayers leaning back with all their weight, the rope taut with faith. They held my exhaustion and effort in their hands.

When I finally lowered, bloody-palmed, tear-streaked, I felt only failure. Until my teammates surrounded me. “You reached the top,” they insisted. It took me hours to understand: the summit wasn’t the point. The triumph was in the trying, in the way my body shook but still pushed, in the chorus of voices that refused to let me quit.

What the Rock Taught Me

  1. Trust is a two way street , it is a reciprocal act. My belayers’ focus mirrored my own vulnerability. To climb is to say: I choose to need you .

  2. Discipline is love, whether for the climb or anything else. Those bleeding hands were proof, not of weakness, but of devotion to the effort.

  3. Community is oxygen. When my strength faltered and lowered down, their belief became my second wind.

That day on the cliff, I learned the difference between failing and being a failure. One is an event; the other is a lie. Now, when faced with any challenge, academic, personal, political, I ask: Am I climbing with the same courage? and with all of what I have. Because rock climbing didn’t just teach me how to ascend. It taught me how to be held.

Arizona, 22 May 2023
