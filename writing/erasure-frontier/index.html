Not Knowing Is the Point

Not knowing is the point. When systems become too complex to understand, responsibility has a way of dissolving. Harm can be acknowledged without anyone being accountable for it. This pattern is not new. Long before artificial intelligence had “black boxes,” colonial institutions perfected ways of making violence disappear behind expertise, progress, and supposedly neutral knowledge.

Before we go further, we need to define the word this essay turns on. Opacity, as we use it here, is not the absence of knowledge. It is the organized production of non-knowledge: structures of invisibility that decide what can be known, by whom, and with what consequences for responsibility. It operates along two axes. One is visibility: what gets displayed, and what gets submerged beneath the display. The other is time, and here is what I would like you to notice about time. Indigenous peoples were fixed statically in the past, so their present claims read as anachronism. AI is accelerated into the future, always about to arrive, its harms always premature to litigate. Opposite directions, and the same result: the subject is removed from the accountable now. We should also be precise about our own moment. The black box shrug is not a danger approaching from the future; it is already offered in courtrooms and press releases as a reason no one in particular is responsible. The task is not prevention. It is slowing a normalization that is already underway.

But there is a distinction we need to make before the argument can carry weight, because there are two different unknowns in play whenever we talk about AI. Call the first mechanism opacity: the inside of the model, billions of parameters that even their builders struggle to trace. Call the second impact opacity: the outside of the model, the diffuse and largely untraceable spread of consequences across land, water, labor, and communities that never appear in any dashboard. A perfect map of a model’s interior would not tell us whose aquifer cools it. Our institutions are working hard on the first opacity and leaving the second intact, and the second is where the frontier lives. Here are the stakes, stated plainly at the start: a transparent system that still concentrates power in a few hands is not accountable. It is a well-lit empire.

Institutionalizing Opacity: From the Abbe Museum to the AI Black Box

Consider when Dr. Robert Abbe opened his museum in Bar Harbor, Maine, in 1928, displaying what he called “the indestructible evidence of a vanished race.” I take this account from Darren Ranco, a Penobscot anthropologist whose nation is among those the museum declared vanished, and his co-author Julia Clark (2015); I rely on their history because it documents the mechanism from inside Wabanaki experience rather than from the settler archive alone. And here is the detail that should stop us. Wabanaki summer encampments occurred in Bar Harbor annually throughout this period. Abbe saw them. The Natives were literally sitting outside. Yet the museum would “not concern ourselves” with “recent Indian life” (p. 57).

Here’s what I would like you to notice: the museum created a “known” history in order to hide a living reality. If a people belonged to a “Stone Age,” their descendants had no claims to territory, and the settler held no responsibility for ongoing dispossession. The display case was, in this sense, the first user interface: it showed everything and disclosed nothing.

You may worry that this is only an analogy, and that worry is fair, so let us put actual machinery behind it. James Muldoon and Boxi Wu, political theorists writing in Philosophy and Technology (2023), map what they call the colonial supply chain of AI: an international division of digital labor that extracts value from majority world workers while discourses of AI’s universality render the extraction invisible. Moving to today’s world, the clean chatbot we enjoy is the exhibit; the Kenyan workers who filtered its most violent training content for a few dollars an hour, documented by Billy Perrigo in TIME (2023), are our era’s encampment down the road. The glass has not changed.

The AI side deserves its say here, and we want to be more than fair to it; we want to be accurate. Dario Amodei, chief executive of Anthropic, whom I cite precisely because he leads one of the companies building these systems, describes modern AI as emergent rather than directly designed, and champions interpretability research (2025): methods for tracing what happens inside a model, something like an MRI for its interior. And there is a concession my own argument owes him. The opacity of these systems is not, in itself, a scheme. It is substantially intrinsic to the method; even a maximally ethical lab, training a large model with the best intentions, ends up holding an artifact it cannot fully explain. So the accusation needs sharpening rather than blurring. The opacity is not the choice. Deploying at planetary scale despite it is the choice, and treating the resulting unaccountability as a permanent technical fact rather than a reversible decision is the move this essay is written against.

But there is another question we need to ask: who benefits when systems cannot be questioned? When Wabanaki people were denied claims to land, officials pointed to displays proving they had “vanished.” Today, when an algorithmic system flags a family for a fraud investigation, institutions point to the black box: we cannot explain why the model decided that. In both cases a zone appears where harm happens and no one is responsible. The museum built its zone deliberately. Ours we built by shipping first and understanding later, which is a different sin with the same beneficiaries.

The Disciplinary Carve-Up: Breaking the World into Fragments by Design

To understand who benefits from AI opacity, we first need to recognize another colonial pattern: fragmentation as a technology of control. Historian Robert Berkhofer (1978) showed that “the Indian” was never a misunderstanding but an invention, “a White invention and still largely a White image” (p. 3). Why invent the fragment? Because you cannot displace the Penobscot, Passamaquoddy, Maliseet, and Mi’kmaq peoples, each with their own sovereignty and relationships to land, with a single uniform policy. But you can displace “the Indian.” Collapse distinct nations into one category and you have a dataset you can administer. Linda Tuhiwai Smith, the Māori scholar whose Decolonizing Methodologies (2012) founded this field, names the next operation: imperialism’s disciplinary carve-up. Colonialism did not just take land; it dismembered entire worlds. “Bones, mummies and skulls to the museums, art work to private collectors, languages to linguistics” (p. 29). Each fragment was filed in its proper discipline, stripped of the relations that gave it meaning. And her deeper point is the one we most need: the filing system decides whose ways of knowing count as knowledge at all.

Now look at how AI systems are trained, and see whether you recognize the filing system. Tokenization severs text from author and circumstance. Feature selection keeps what predicts and discards what situates. The loss function has no term for provenance, consent, or relation, so the optimization is formally indifferent to them, and the indifference persists even though context-keeping alternatives exist by name, from dataset documentation standards to consent-based corpora. It is not that we cannot preserve context. It is that context preservation was never the design goal. And the cabinet has a history. These systems are grown from the written archive, which is not a neutral sample of humanity; it was assembled disproportionately during centuries of empire and carries the categorization regimes it was built to administer. Train on the archive of empire, and the model inherits more than information.

Here’s the crucial tension I want you to see, because the strongest objection to all of this comes from inside AI itself, and we should meet it rather than avoid it. Richard Sutton, among the field’s most influential learning theorists, argues in his “bitter lesson” (2019) that encoded human knowledge always loses, eventually, to general methods that learn from raw experience and computation. Read one way, that is the carve-up perfected: human context demoted from stolen good to noise. But read again, and Sutton becomes an accidental ally. His ideal learner is not a scraped archive at all; it is a situated agent that knows by doing, in an environment, over time, which is structurally closer to knowing-in-relation than anything currently shipping. The disagreement that remains is real, and we will state it rather than settle it: in kinship frameworks, relation precedes and governs intelligence; for the scaling thesis, relation is a bottleneck. Notice that today’s systems satisfy neither side. That should worry everyone.

The Digital Frontier: The Extraction of Power Without Accountability

When settler colonialism seeks power without accountability, it creates zones where normal rules do not apply. Alfonso Ortiz, the Ohkay Owingeh anthropologist whose 1988 essay views the frontier “from the other side,” offers the crucial framework, and I follow him because he refuses the settler camera angle. The frontier, he argues, was never a line of advancing civilization; viewed from the other side it was a zone of invasion and “regeneration through violence” (p. 11), where moral obligation was suspended in the name of progress. Invasion is his word, and we will keep it in his mouth. Here’s why this matters for AI: for our moment the quieter verb is seepage. AI does not storm the settlement; it saturates it, arriving through defaults and terms nobody reads, until one day it is the environment. The tempo differs. The relationship to consent does not.

We also need to name the frontier’s favorite costume, because the word “digital” is doing opacity work. It makes one of history’s largest industrial buildouts sound like weather, and it is not weather. Honor the Earth, the Indigenous-led environmental organization, launched a No Data Centers Coalition in 2025 and tracks on the order of a hundred or more proposed hyperscale data centers on or near Native lands. Organizers describe a recurring choreography: a corporation arrives talking about solar, the talk turns to a hyperscale data center, and before substance is discussed, Tribal leadership is asked to sign a nondisclosure agreement, becoming accountable to the corporation rather than to their own citizens. Notice what that instrument is. The NDA is opacity produced by signature rather than complexity: the frontier and the black box in a single document.

What happened at the Abbe Museum is happening, concretely, across Maine, so let us keep a local ledger. The first entry is the courts. In Penobscot Nation v. Frey, the Nation asked the federal courts to affirm that the Penobscot River, its namesake, the water its members have always fished, is part of its reservation. In 2021 the First Circuit, sitting en banc, held that the reservation consists of the islands’ uplands only, resting on the dictionary definition of “island” as a piece of land, and the Supreme Court declined review. Read that slowly: a dictionary, the archive at its most distilled, was used to sever a people from the water that constitutes them, over their own account of their own boundaries. Erasure by definition, in both senses of the phrase. The second entry is the legislature. In April 2026 Maine passed LD 307, the nation’s first statewide data center moratorium; Governor Mills vetoed it while conceding in writing that a moratorium is appropriate, because the bill would not exempt a 550 million dollar project at a shuttered mill in Jay, and the override failed by a handful of votes. Lewiston’s council had already voted down a 300 million dollar center after community opposition; Bangor paused for six months to think; and small towns nationally are compiling the same playbook, as the Michigan Climate Action Network’s reporting on data center accountability shows.

The third entry cuts against my own argument, which is exactly why it stays. Jay wanted the data center. A mill town gutted by closure saw jobs and a tax base, and its officials asked the governor to protect the project. If consent means anything, it includes the right to say yes, and a framework that celebrates Lewiston’s no while dismissing Jay’s yes as false consciousness is not a consent framework; it is a preference wearing one. What the ledger teaches is narrower and harder: jurisdiction, not outcome, is the value. The failure mode is not a town choosing a data center. It is a state’s declared caution outweighed by a single carve-out, and communities discovering the terms only after the signature. The frontier does not need to win the argument. It needs the decision moved out of the community’s reach.

Where the Parallel Breaks Down: The Limits of Technical Solutions

However, the parallel strains, and we must be careful not to conflate the technical challenges of AI with the lived violence of colonization, because recognizing a pattern is not claiming equivalence. Eve Tuck, an Unangax̄ scholar, and K. Wayne Yang wrote the essay that polices this entire genre, “Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor” (2012): decolonization means land and life, not a synonym for improvement, and they insist on an “ethic of incommensurability” (p. 28). Centuries of dispossession and ongoing sovereignty struggles are incommensurable with a software industry’s design failures. This is why our title says settler colonialism teaches us, not decolonization: the colonial technology of opacity is the teacher, while decolonial thought is the allied standpoint we borrow, with acknowledgment, to see emergent harm early. The pattern is shared. The wound is not.

And the sharpest version of this warning targets our exact subject. Rachel Adams, a South African scholar of race and technology (2021), asks whether AI can be decolonized at all, and cautions that when the call does not address race and historically embedded colonial power, “decolonization is being misappropriated as a metaphor.” I cite her because she is the reader most likely to object to this essay, and we would rather host the objection than receive it. Her test cuts at interpretability too. Tuck and Yang describe settler “moves to innocence,” strategies that relieve guilt without surrendering power, and interpretability risks becoming one: a way to feel ethical because you understand your model’s bias, while ceding no power over whether and where it deploys. Understanding the mechanism while retaining the empire is the well-lit empire we began with.

Toward a Decolonial Interpretability: The Relational Corrective

This is where we can return to the Abbe Museum with new questions. The failure was never informational; Abbe had the information, and he saw the encampments. The failure was relational: a refusal of the obligations that seeing creates. If we truly want to advance AI in a way that benefits humanity, we need a different framework for what knowing even is, and this is where Daniel Wildcat (2009) becomes essential. Wildcat, a Yuchi member of the Muscogee Nation, argues in Red Alert! that Western technocratic solutions fail because they treat problems as isolated “glitches” to be fixed. Indigenous knowledge, he counters, is fundamentally relational: we must “understand our lives as essentially not only about us, but about our human selves in… the web of life” (p. 5). Knowledge cannot be separated from responsibility. Wildcat also hands us an instrument, and I want us to actually use it. Call it the Wildcat test: not “how does this model work,” but “what relationships does this model create or destroy?” The MRI looks inward at mechanism. The Wildcat test looks outward at relation. It is the diagnostic for impact opacity.

Two things follow, one demanding and one generous. The demanding one: the test discriminates. The same computational substrate that powers hyperscale extraction also powers small, locally governed ecological models, forecasting harmful algal blooms and shellfish toxins so that coastal harvesters on this very coast know when the flats are safe. Who owns it, who consented, what relations does it maintain? The two systems return different answers, which means the test succeeds where “AI good, AI bad” fails. Now the generous one, and it rebalances this essay: the Wildcat test cannot run blind. We cannot assess what relationships a system destroys if no one can characterize what the system does. Mechanism interpretability and relational interpretability need each other; Amodei’s MRI is necessary for the very accountability his industry’s deployment schedule undermines. Our quarrel was never with the instrument. It is with mistaking the instrument for the accounting.

There is one more distinction we owe the reader, because a careful critic will notice an apparent contradiction: we have condemned opacity for six pages, and we are about to celebrate it. The Martinican philosopher Édouard Glissant defended what he called the right to opacity: the right of colonized peoples not to be fully known, measured, and made legible on the colonizer’s terms. That right is alive in this essay’s own examples. The Maine Department of Education states that generative AI cannot use its Wabanaki Studies materials, because the materials were created in collaboration with Wabanaki knowledge-sharers and AI lacks the lived relation vital to them. That refusal is opacity, and it is just. So the moral variable was never opacity versus transparency. It is the direction of legibility. Colonialism’s signature is the asymmetry: power stays opaque while rendering its subjects transparent, surveilled, catalogued, displayed in vitrines. Justice runs the other way: power legible to the people it affects, and communities entitled to their own opacity. Interpretability is good when it points at power. Opacity is good when it is held by the vulnerable. Every example in this essay sorts cleanly under that rule, including the NDA, which fails it twice.

Nor is the relational insight one region’s property, and hearing it in a second register strengthens it rather than diluting it. Ouma Peter Odhiambo, a scholar at the University of Nairobi, describes machine learning arriving in African contexts as an imported “algorithmic gaze” that overwrites relational knowledge with atomized metrics; his fintech case shows Nairobi lenders redefining trust from a communal virtue into a calculated probability, and his corrective, grounded in the Ubuntu ethic of relationality, is a shift from data subjects to data stewards. We set him beside Wildcat carefully: two distinct traditions, not a monolith, independently diagnosing the same subtraction and independently prescribing the same cure, which is jurisdiction. Even the laboratory now says a version of this. Shakir Mohamed, Marie-Therese Png, and William Isaac, writing from within DeepMind (2020), propose “reverse tutelage”: AI communities learning from the communities they affect. When the critique is audible inside the lab, the excuse that it is external to the field expires. And the spectrum of jurisdiction is already visible in practice, running from the Maine DOE’s refusal, through the CARE Principles’ access on community terms, to Te Hiku Media, the Māori broadcaster whose speech models keep the language’s data under the language community’s authority. The question was never whether Indigenous knowledge enters computational systems. It is who sets the terms.

Unsettling the Future: From Transparency to Jurisdiction

So where does this leave us? The measures we need are not new; they descend from decades of environmental justice praxis and Indigenous data sovereignty movements. Consent before data collection, in the meaning the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples gives it, including the clause the industry keeps omitting: the right to say no, which, as Jay reminds us, only means anything because it travels with the right to say yes. Interpretability research that asks affected communities what they need explained, not just what developers wish to explain. Regulatory frameworks that reject “it’s a black box” as a legal defense, so that the consequences of deploying an unexplainable system attach to the deployer. Revenue shared with the communities whose knowledge, labor, water, and watts train the models. And energy and water accounting attached to a veto, because visibility without jurisdiction is a well-lit empire with better paperwork. These are not additions to interpretability. Understood from Indigenous and decolonial perspectives, they are the interpretability: the outward-facing half that no MRI can supply.

One honesty remains, and we will not landscape over it. It is possible that hyperscale AI as currently constituted is simply incommensurable with these obligations, that no consent protocol survives contact with a hundred billion dollar buildout. We do not resolve that tension here; resolving it on paper would be one more move to innocence. We hand it to you intact, with a condition that is ours rather than the industry’s. If these systems are to exist in right relation with the communities and lands that sustain them, the work is not merely making the black box transparent. It is asking who built the box, who it serves, and who holds the standing to refuse it. The goal is not to see the mechanism. It is to redistribute the power the mechanism wields.
