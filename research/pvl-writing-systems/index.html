Preferred Viewing Location Outside the Latin Alphabet

2026

Al Madi Lab, Colby College

  • analysis code (lab repo)

You are reading this in English, left to right, and your eye is not doing what you think it is doing. It is not landing at the start of each word. It lands inside the word, left of centre, roughly a third of the way in, and it has been doing that in the literature since Rayner measured it in 1979. The effect is called the Preferred Viewing Location, and it is load-bearing: models of reading are built on top of where the eye chooses to go.

A related result, the Optimal Viewing Position, comes from O’Regan and colleagues in the 1980s and is a different claim, though the two are constantly conflated. The PVL is where the eye does land. The OVP is where recognition is best, and it sits nearer the middle of the word.

Now notice what the evidence for both is made of. English, French, German, Dutch. Alphabets, all of them written left to right, all of them decomposing into tidy letter positions. The eye lands a third of the way in from the left, because that is where the eye lands in the languages the field happened to study first.

I grew up reading in the other direction. So the question I wanted to ask was not whether the result is true, but whether it is a result about reading or a result about the Latin alphabet.

With Naser Al Madi, whose lab I work in, I went through the Multilingual Eye-Tracking dataset for eight writing systems: English, Spanish, Russian, Kazakh, Vietnamese, Hindi in Devanagari, Urdu in Nastaliq, and source code. Nastaliq is the one that earns the study. It runs right to left, it is heavily ligatured, and a word in it does not break into discrete letter slots the way an alphabet does. If the classic result is about the mechanics of the eye, it should survive the crossing. If it is about the shape of Latin words, it should not.

Here is what makes the finding worth the trouble. Take the average landing position in each of the eight languages and they cluster in a narrow band. On the mean alone the effect looks universal, which is exactly how a field keeps believing something for forty years. Look instead at where the eye lands most often, the peak of the distribution rather than its centre of mass, and the languages come apart. Some peak near the classic third. Some peak past the middle of the word. Some peak very early.

The averages were hiding it.

I am still writing this up, so the numbers stay with the paper and this is the shape of the result rather than the result. The methodological point is the part I would defend now: across eight languages the mean and the mode tell different stories, and the field has mostly been reporting the mean.
